    Competitions

    A deadline and an audience change everything. Learners who drift on open-ended projects often transform when there's a date, a rulebook and other teams.

    UK-focused, but most of these run internationally.

    Robotics

    Pi Wars — ages 11+ and adults

    Raspberry Pi robots competing in non-destructive challenges, both autonomous and remote-controlled. Held over a weekend in Cambridge, with school, family, hobbyist and solo categories. Open to entrants worldwide. The YouTube channel has years of past runs, and competitor blogs are an excellent free build-log resource.

    Why it's good: the challenge variety rewards different strengths, the community is unusually welcoming, and the barrier to a competitive entry is low. Probably the best first competition for a UK Pi-based team.

    FIRST LEGO League — ages 9–16

    ⚠️ The 2026–2027 BIOGLOW season is the final one. LEGO Education has not renewed its partnership with FIRST. Two editions run in parallel: Founders Edition on SPIKE hardware (final season 2027–2028) and Future Edition on the new Computer Science & AI kits. LEGO Education plans its own separate competition from 2027–2028.

    Season materials — rubrics, scoresheets, judging flowcharts and twelve support sessions — are free to download and useful as a structure even outside the competition.

    VEX Robotics Competition — ages 8–18+

    VEX IQ (primary/lower secondary) and VEX V5 (upper secondary). Metal construction system, well-established, and now the strongest continuing option for schools leaving FLL. Runs through RoboticsEducation.org.

    FIRST Tech Challenge / FIRST Robotics Competition — ages 12–18

    Where FLL teams are now pointed. FRC in particular is a serious commitment — significant budget, a six-week build season, and a large mentor team — but the payoff is correspondingly large.

    RoboCup Junior — ages up to 19

    Soccer, rescue and OnStage leagues. Platform-agnostic, so a Pico or Arduino robot is entirely viable. Strong international pathway.

    Engineering & design

    Tomorrow's Engineers EEP Robotics Challenge — UK, ages 11–14

    Free to enter for UK schools, LEGO-based, and designed to need no prior robotics experience from the teacher. A good first step for a school with no existing programme.

    F1 in Schools — ages 9–19

    CAD, CFD, manufacturing, and a business case alongside the engineering. Broader than pure coding, and reaches learners who want the design and communication side.

    Formula Student — university level

    Where this pathway ends up. Worth showing to 16-year-olds as a destination.

    Space

    Astro Pi — ages up to 19, free

    Write Python that runs on Raspberry Pi hardware aboard the ISS. Two tiers: Mission Zero takes a single lesson and every valid entry runs in space, which makes it the single highest-impact-per-hour activity in this entire repository. Mission Space Lab is a serious multi-month research project.

    Run by the Raspberry Pi Foundation with ESA. Free. If you do one thing from this page, do Mission Zero.

    ESA CanSat — ages 14–19

    Build a satellite the size of a drinks can, launch it to ~1 km, recover and analyse the telemetry. National rounds including a UK competition, then a European final.

    UK Rocketry Challenge — ages 11–18

    Design, build and fly a rocket to a target altitude with a fragile payload.

    Coding (no hardware)

    • Advent of Code — December daily puzzles, free, addictive, all languages
    • Bebras — UK computational thinking challenge, ages 6–18, free
    • UK Bebras → Oxford Computing Challenge → British Informatics Olympiad — the competitive programming pathway
    • Coolest Projects — Raspberry Pi Foundation showcase rather than a competition. No winners, no losers, any project accepted. Excellent for learners who freeze under competitive pressure.

    Choosing one

    If your group is… Try
    A school with no existing programme, no budget Astro Pi Mission Zero, then Bebras
    A school with LEGO already FLL Founders Edition while it lasts, then VEX IQ
    A hobbyist family or small club Pi Wars
    Ambitious 16–18s wanting a personal statement CanSat, Astro Pi Mission Space Lab, FRC
    Learners who dislike competition Coolest Projects

    Running a team: what actually helps

    • Start earlier than feels necessary. Every experienced coach says this and every new coach ignores it.
    • Build two of everything critical. Robots break the week before. Spare motors and a spare controller cost less than a season.
    • Log everything. Photos, decisions, failed attempts. Most competitions score the engineering journal or judged presentation heavily, and it's the part teams leave until last. It's also the part that most resembles real engineering.
    • Let them lose properly. The learning is in the debrief. Coaches who build the robot produce teams who learned nothing and know it.
    • Watch past runs on YouTube. Pi Wars and FLL both have extensive archives. An hour of watching saves a month of guessing.
